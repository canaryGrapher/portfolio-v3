# New Delhi Doesn't Need More Roads. It Needs Straight Lines.

**The cheapest fix for the capital region's traffic is already painted on the tarmac. We just refuse to use it.**

Stand on the Delhi-Gurugram Expressway at 9:15 on a Tuesday morning and count the lanes. Then count the columns of vehicles actually moving down them. The second number is always higher. Eight painted lanes routinely carry ten or eleven ragged files of traffic, each one a few inches from the next, each one drifting sideways whenever a gap appears. Motorcycles thread the seams. A truck sits in the extreme right lane doing 40. An SUV crosses four lanes in one move to make an exit it spotted late.

We have been told for two decades that this is a capacity problem. So we build. Flyovers at Rajokri, underpasses in Noida, elevated corridors in Ghaziabad, an expressway to Meerut, another to Dwarka. And every year the numbers get worse. Delhi crossed 87.6 lakh registered vehicles in 2025-26, a rise of nearly 8 percent in a single year, at a density of 522 vehicles per thousand people. TomTom's index put New Delhi at a 60 percent congestion level, with 104 hours lost per driver in rush hour. The Centre for Science and Environment found something more damning still: on Delhi's arterial roads, peak and non-peak speeds have converged at around 26 to 27 km/h. There is no longer an off-peak. The jam is now the baseline condition.

Here is the uncomfortable proposition. A meaningful share of that congestion is not caused by too many vehicles for the asphalt. It is caused by the way those vehicles occupy the asphalt. NCR does not have a road shortage. It has a queuing disorder. And unlike almost every other intervention on the table, fixing it costs close to nothing.

## What lane indiscipline actually does to a road

Traffic engineers have a term for the thing that ruins a highway: capacity drop. A stretch of road has a theoretical throughput. Push it near that limit and it holds. Introduce a disturbance, one vehicle braking to let another squeeze in, and the queue behind it decelerates in a wave that travels backwards and persists long after the original disturbance is gone. Throughput after the breakdown is measurably lower than throughput before it. The road loses capacity precisely when it is needed most.

Lane changing is the disturbance generator. Every unnecessary merge is a braking event for someone behind. When traffic runs in loose, overlapping streams rather than defined lanes, the entire carriageway becomes one continuous merge. There is no such thing as a settled queue, so the wave never dissipates.

The Indian evidence is not merely theoretical. A field study of dispersing traffic at a level crossing found that when the same volume of vehicles queued with lane discipline versus without, average delay fell from 574 seconds to 272 seconds, dispersal time dropped from 84 seconds to 33, and average dispersing speed nearly doubled, from 3.78 m/s to 7.26 m/s. Same road. Same vehicles. Same signal. The only variable was whether the queue was organised or a mob.

Scale that intuition to the Ashram junction, the DND toll plaza, the Kherki Daula stretch, the Noida Sector 62 flyover approach. The bottleneck is not the width. It is the fact that fifty vehicles arriving at a four-lane opening as eight fanned-out columns must resolve themselves into four, at zero speed, one anxious nudge at a time.

There is a second effect, less discussed and arguably worse. Lane indiscipline destroys predictability, and predictability is what allows drivers to maintain speed. If I cannot be reasonably sure that the vehicle to my left will stay to my left, I must drive defensively, which means slowly, which means leaving a gap I do not want to leave, which someone will immediately fill. Everyone driving cautiously in an unpredictable system produces slower aggregate flow than everyone driving briskly in a predictable one. The chaos is self-reinforcing.

## The objections, and why they don't survive contact

**"Indian traffic is heterogeneous. Lanes don't work with cycles, autos and trucks mixing."** This is the most serious argument and it deserves a real answer. It is true that a lane sized for a car wastes space when a two-wheeler uses it, and true that strict lane-following in mixed traffic can reduce raw throughput on some sections. But heterogeneity is an argument for *lane assignment by vehicle class*, not for abandoning lanes. Reserve the left lanes for slow and heavy vehicles, keep the right for overtaking, allow two-wheelers a defined kerbside channel, and you get the ordering benefit without pretending a scooter is a sedan. Haryana's own directive already requires heavy vehicles to keep to the second-most-left lane on four-lane highways. The concept is accepted. The enforcement is not.

**"Our roads are too badly designed for lane driving."** Partly fair. Lane markings across NCR are faded, disappear entirely at junctions, and vanish under construction detours. Lanes drop without warning. Bus stops sit mid-carriageway. You cannot ask citizens to follow lines that do not exist. But this is a case for fixing the lines, which costs a fraction of a flyover, not for giving up on them.

**"Enforcement is impossible."** This one is contradicted by NCR's own geography. A Tribune report on Gurugram's struggle with lane driving noted something telling: compliance improves noticeably the moment vehicles cross into Delhi, where dedicated enforcement operations run. Same drivers. Same vehicles. Different expectation of consequence. Behaviour is not a fixed national trait. It is a response to enforcement probability.

## Why nobody is doing it

Look at what Delhi Traffic Police actually enforced in a recent month. The top on-the-spot categories were improper parking, missing pollution certificates, no helmet, no licence, no insurance. The top camera-detected violations were overspeeding, improper parking, no helmet, stop-line violation, red-light jumping. Over two lakh overspeeding challans in a single month, on roads averaging 26 km/h.

Lane violation appears nowhere near the top of either list. In Gurugram, a district crossed by four highways, one report counted 158 lane challans in a month.

This is not an oversight. It is the natural output of an enforcement system optimised for what cameras can trivially detect and what fine schedules make easy to prosecute. A speed camera measures a number. A red-light camera detects a binary. Lane discipline requires observing a vehicle's behaviour over distance, and until recently that meant a constable with a judgment call, which meant discretion, which meant friction. So the violation with arguably the largest aggregate effect on congestion became the one nobody writes.

That excuse is expiring. NCR is already blanketed in ANPR cameras, and lane-keeping detection is a solved computer-vision problem, deployed routinely on European motorways. The National Highways Authority has been sitting on approvals for automated lane cameras on the Gurugram expressway. Approve them. The marginal cost on infrastructure that already exists is software and mounting brackets.

## What a serious programme looks like

It is not complicated, which is exactly why the failure to do it is indefensible.

Repaint every arterial road in NCR to a single specification, with markings that survive the monsoon and continue through junctions rather than politely disappearing at them. Publish a lane-assignment rule by vehicle class and post it on gantries every kilometre. Enable automated lane-violation detection on existing camera infrastructure, starting with the twenty worst bottlenecks in the region. Make the first offence an educational notice and the second one expensive. Enforce it identically across Delhi, Gurugram, Noida, Ghaziabad and Faridabad, because a rule that changes at a state border is not a rule, it is a suggestion with a jurisdiction.

And measure it. Pick five corridors, instrument them, publish before-and-after speeds every quarter. If lane enforcement does not move the numbers, we will know within two seasons and can stop. No flyover in NCR has ever been held to that standard.

## The real argument

Lane driving will not solve NCR's traffic on its own. Nothing will. The region needs the metro extensions, the bus fleet it keeps promising, the parking pricing it keeps deferring, and eventually the congestion charge it is terrified to discuss.

But every one of those takes years and thousands of crores. Lane discipline takes paint, cameras we have already bought, and the political will to enforce an existing law. Peak-hour congestion in four Indian cities including Delhi was estimated years ago to cost the economy around 22 billion dollars a year. Against that, a regional repainting and enforcement programme is a rounding error.

We have spent a generation treating congestion as a construction problem because construction is visible, ribbon-cuttable and procurable. Order is none of those things. It is just cheaper, faster and, on the evidence, more effective.

The lanes are already there. We are simply refusing to stand in them.
